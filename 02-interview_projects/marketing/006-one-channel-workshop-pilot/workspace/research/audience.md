# Small audience interview digest

Six fictional interview sessions were conducted internally on 2026-10-28. They were not a representative market study and no participant gave permission for a public quote. Three participants were application developers, two were release coordinators and one was a platform administrator. Their individual names were removed before this digest. Do not turn these counts into market percentages.

## Recurring needs

The developers wanted to see changed field names and an example of a useful review question. They did not want a generic article on all of application security. Two found the old “hacking lab” title intimidating because their role was release coordination, not testing attacks. The administrator worried that joining would require uploading a real production configuration. The workshop uses fictional prepared examples; this is a valuable detail for invitations and FAQs.

## Channel context

The newsletter reaches existing subscribers who already know the company name. A community board reaches people asking practical questions, but its rules allow an educational answer and one relevant link, with no sales pitch or repeated posts. The company blog is a more suitable home for a durable explanation. We have no evidence that the channels reach disjoint people. A small campaign should choose where it can provide a specific useful answer, not assume that every channel needs the same text.

## Questions still open

We do not know whether the next audience prefers a morning session. We do not have an approved benchmark for time saved. The team can ask one short feedback question after the workshop, provided it is voluntary and does not collect sensitive configuration details. A plan can record these as learning goals; it cannot turn them into already-known customer preferences.

## Internal content examples

One participant described a review as “a detective story with too many spreadsheets.” This is an internal observation, not a quotable testimonial. Another asked if a screenshot could prove a release was safe. The answer is no: the review aid highlights changes and a human assesses context. These notes can motivate a plain-language explanation without repeating private words in public copy.
